This assumes you know [the rules](/guide/rules) and [how the wall scores](/guide/scoring). Everything here follows from one fact: a tile's value depends entirely on what is already next to it.

## 1. Build outward from one cluster, not in five directions

Your first two or three settled tiles are almost worthless whatever you do — they score 1 each. What matters is whether they are near each other. Pick a corner of your wall and commit to it. Tiles four and five then score 2 and 3 instead of 1 and 1, and by the second half of the game each new tile is joining two runs at once.

The common mistake is drafting whatever color happens to be plentiful and letting the wall fill in scattershot. That produces a board full of settled tiles and a score in the twenties.

## 2. Price the floor line, do not fear it

Overflow is not a mistake; it is a cost. Work out the actual trade before deciding:

- Two overflow tiles cost you a few points and clear at the end of the round.
- A completed line that lands beside two existing tiles can easily score 5 or more.

Take the penalty when it completes something. Refuse it when it does not. The players who never touch the floor line are usually leaving more points on the table than the players who overpay for it.

## 3. Remember that taking is also giving

Every factory draw pushes the leftovers into the center. Taking one green off a factory holding a green, a blue, a yellow and a red hands your opponent three usable tiles in a single pile. Before committing, look at what your draw creates for the other side — sometimes the same tiles are available from a source that spills less, and sometimes a slightly worse tile for you is much worse for them.

## 4. Deny the line you can read

Pattern lines are public. A four-slot line holding two whites is a visible announcement that your opponent needs exactly two more whites and can accept nothing else there. If whites are scarce, taking them is often worth more than whatever you would have taken instead — especially late, when there is no round left for them to recover in.

Because the Daily is scored on **margin**, denial counts twice. A move that costs you 3 and costs them 9 improves your number by 6.

## 5. Play the endgame for columns

Once anyone is one tile from completing a horizontal row, count the ending. A row is worth +2 and ends the game. A column is worth +7, and a full color set +10.

Two questions decide the last two rounds:

1. **Do I want the game to end now?** If your opponent is a tile away from a column, ending it early is worth more than the row bonus. If you are the one with a column in reach, stall.
2. **Which single tile is worth most?** Late in the game the answer is usually the one that completes a column, not the one that completes a line.

## 6. Take the "1" marker on purpose

The first-player marker costs −1 and buys the lead next round. It is worth it in exactly one situation, but that situation is common: a color you need is running short, and leading means you get first claim on it. Taking it by accident, on a round where nothing is scarce, is just −1.

## 7. On the Daily, play the deal, not the clock

Everyone gets the same deal, and the board ranks on margin first — time is only the tiebreaker. Thinking for an extra twenty seconds about which corner of the wall to commit to is worth far more than the seconds it costs. Speed matters when two players have the same margin, which is not most of the time.
