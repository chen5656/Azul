This assumes you know [the rules](/guide/rules) and [how memory scores](/guide/scoring). Everything here follows from one fundamental fact: a token's value in memory depends entirely on what is already adjacent to it.

## 1. Build outward from one memory cluster, not in five directions

Your first two or three crystallized tokens are almost isolated whatever you do — they score 1 point each. What matters is whether they are near each other. Pick a corner of your memory grid and commit to it. Tokens four and five then score 2 and 3 instead of 1 and 1, and by the second half of the duel each new token joins two clusters at once.

The common mistake is drafting whatever color happens to be plentiful and letting your memory fill in scattershot. That produces a board full of isolated tokens and a score in the twenties.

## 2. Price the hallucination, do not fear it

Overflow is not a mistake; it is a calculated cognitive cost. Work out the actual trade before deciding:

- Two overflow tokens cost you a few points of hallucination and clear at the end of the round.
- A completed context line that crystallizes beside two existing memory nodes can easily score 5 or more points.

Take the hallucination penalty when it completes something significant. Refuse it when it does not. Players who never touch the hallucination line are usually leaving more points on the table than players who overpay for it.

## 3. Remember that taking is also feeding the buffer

Every attention draw pushes the leftovers into the central buffer. Taking one green off an attention node holding a green, a blue, a yellow and a red hands your opponent three usable tokens in a single buffer draw. Before committing, look at what your draw creates for the AI — sometimes the same tokens are available from an attention node that spills less, and sometimes a slightly suboptimal token for you creates a catastrophic overflow for the AI.

## 4. Intercept the context line you can read

Context lines are completely public across the neural link. A four-slot line holding two whites is a visible announcement that your opponent needs exactly two more whites and can accept nothing else there. If whites are scarce, intercepting them is often worth more than whatever you would have taken instead — especially late, when there is no time left to recover.

Because the Daily Challenge is scored on **margin**, cognitive interception counts twice. A move that costs you 3 points and costs the AI 9 points improves your net leaderboard margin by 6.

## 5. Play the endgame for memory columns

Once either side is one token away from completing a horizontal row, count the endgame value. A row is worth +2 and ends the game. A column is worth +7, and a full color set +10.

Two questions decide the final rounds:

1. **Do I want convergence to happen now?** If your AI opponent is a token away from completing a high-value column, triggering convergence early is worth more than waiting. If you are the one with a column in reach, stall.
2. **Which single token is worth most?** Late in the game the answer is usually the one that completes a memory column, not the one that merely fills a line.

## 6. Claim the initiative marker on purpose

The initiative ("1") marker in the buffer costs −1 in hallucination and buys the first action next round. It is worth it when a critical color frequency is running short and you need first claim on it. Taking it by accident on a round where nothing is scarce is simply wasting a point of mental stability.

## 7. On the Daily, play the puzzle, not the clock

Everyone faces the identical attention deal, and the leaderboard ranks on margin first — elapsed time is only the tiebreaker. Taking an extra twenty seconds to plan memory clustering is worth far more than the seconds it costs.
